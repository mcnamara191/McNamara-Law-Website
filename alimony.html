Alimony · Spousal Support

Alimony in Florida has changed. Know where you stand.

Florida ended permanent alimony in 2023. Courts can now award temporary, bridge-the-gap, rehabilitative, or durational alimony, based on one spouse's need and the other's ability to pay.

Whether you expect to pay alimony or need it, the 2023 reform changed what is realistic. McNamara Law advises both paying and receiving spouses across Central Florida and negotiates and litigates alimony as part of a divorce or a later modification.

Types of Alimony

Four forms of support under current law

Before awarding any alimony, the court must find that one spouse has an actual need for support and the other has the ability to pay. If both are shown, the judge decides which form or combination fits the circumstances.

Available Today

Forms of alimony in Florida

  • Temporary alimony. Support while the divorce case is pending
  • Bridge-the-gap. Short-term help with the transition to single life, for up to two years
  • Rehabilitative. Support while a spouse gains education, training, or work experience under a specific plan, generally for up to five years
  • Durational. Support for a set period after a marriage of sufficient length, limited by how long the marriage lasted
Amount and Duration

How much, and for how long

Florida has no simple formula for alimony, but the 2023 law added limits. Durational alimony is generally unavailable for marriages of less than three years, and its length is capped at a percentage of the length of the marriage: generally 50% for marriages under 10 years, 60% for 10 to 20 years, and 75% for 20 years or more.

The amount of durational alimony is generally limited to the recipient's reasonable need or 35% of the difference between the spouses' net incomes, whichever is less.

  • Length of the marriage and the standard of living during it
  • Age and health of each spouse
  • Income, assets, and earning capacity of each spouse
  • Contributions to the marriage, including homemaking, child care, and supporting the other's career
  • Responsibilities for minor children
  • Adultery and its economic impact may be considered
Changing or Ending Alimony

Alimony is not always final

Many alimony awards can be modified or terminated when circumstances change substantially. Bridge-the-gap alimony is the main exception and generally cannot be modified.

The 2023 reform also added specific rules for reducing or ending alimony when the paying spouse reaches a reasonable retirement age.

  • Remarriage of the recipient or the death of either spouse generally ends alimony
  • A supportive relationship. Alimony can be reduced or terminated when the recipient lives in a supportive relationship with another person
  • Retirement of the paying spouse, under the statutory factors
  • A substantial change in income or need. How modifications work →
  • Taxes. For divorces finalized after 2018, alimony is generally not deductible by the payer or taxable to the recipient under federal law
