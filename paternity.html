Paternity · Unmarried Parents

For unmarried parents, rights begin with paternity.

When parents are not married, paternity must be legally established before a Florida court can order timesharing or child support. It can be established by a signed acknowledgment or by a court case.

McNamara Law represents fathers who want a legal relationship and time with their child, and mothers who need paternity and support established. A paternity case results in the same kind of parenting plan and support order married parents receive in a divorce.

Why It Matters

What establishing paternity does

When a child is born to married parents, the law presumes the husband is the father. When parents are unmarried, legal fatherhood has to be established. Until it is, a court cannot enter an order giving the father a timesharing schedule or requiring him to pay support.

Establishing paternity benefits everyone involved, most of all the child.

  • For fathers: the ability to obtain a court-ordered parenting plan, timesharing, and a say in major decisions
  • For mothers: child support, shared responsibility for health insurance and expenses
  • For children: financial support, inheritance rights, benefits, and access to family medical history
How Paternity Is Established

Three common paths

The right path depends on whether both parents agree about who the father is and whether anyone disputes it.

Ways to Establish Paternity

Agreement, agency, or court

  • Voluntary acknowledgment. Both parents sign a sworn acknowledgment of paternity, often at the hospital. It can be rescinded for a short period and then becomes binding
  • Administrative process. The Florida Department of Revenue can establish paternity through genetic testing in support cases
  • Court action. Either parent can file a petition to determine paternity. The judge can order genetic testing and decide all related issues in one case
  • Marriage. If the parents marry after the birth, paternity can be established through the marriage and an updated birth record
After Paternity

A birth certificate is not a parenting plan

Many fathers assume that being named on the birth certificate gives them a right to a schedule with their child. Establishing paternity makes you the legal father, but only a court order creates an enforceable timesharing schedule. Without one, there is nothing for a judge to enforce if the other parent refuses contact.

In a paternity case, the court can decide everything at once.

  • Parental responsibility and timesharing, under the same best-interest standard used in divorce. More →
  • Child support, including retroactive support for up to 24 months in many cases. More →
  • Health insurance and uncovered medical expenses
  • The child's name, where a change is requested and appropriate
  • Disestablishment. A man who learns he is not the biological father may be able to ask a court to end his legal obligations under specific conditions
