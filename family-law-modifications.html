Modification of Child Support · Parenting Plans · Timesharing

When life changes, your court order can change too.

A Florida child support order or parenting plan can be modified when there has been a substantial change in circumstances since the last order. Until a judge changes it, the existing order must be followed.

Jobs change, parents move, and children grow. McNamara Law helps Central Florida parents modify child support, parenting plans, and timesharing schedules, and defends parents against modifications that are not justified.

Modification of Child Support

Changing the support amount

Either parent can ask to raise or lower child support when circumstances have changed substantially since the last order. As a general guide, the new guideline amount must differ from the current amount by at least 15% or $50 a month, whichever is greater.

Timing matters. A court can generally change support only back to the date the petition to modify was filed, so every month of delay is a month at the old amount.

  • A significant, lasting change in income for either parent
  • Job loss or disability that is not voluntary
  • A change in the timesharing schedule or the number of overnights
  • Changes in health insurance or childcare costs
  • A child aging out when the order covers more than one child
Modification of Parenting Plans & Timesharing

Changing the schedule or decision-making

To modify a parenting plan, a parent must show a substantial and material change in circumstances since the last order, and that the proposed change is in the child's best interests. Ordinary disagreements and minor schedule frustrations are not enough.

Courts value stability for children, so these cases depend on clear evidence of what has changed and why a new arrangement would be better for the child.

Common Grounds

Changes courts take seriously

  • A parent's relocation or a major change in work schedule
  • The child's needs have changed with age, school, or health
  • Safety concerns such as substance abuse, neglect, or domestic violence
  • Repeated interference with the other parent's time
  • The plan no longer reflects reality because the parents have followed a different schedule for a long time
Agreements and Enforcement

Put every change in a court order

Parents often agree informally to change a schedule or a support amount. Those side agreements are generally not enforceable, and the original order still controls. A parent who relied on a handshake deal can end up owing thousands in back support.

When both parents agree, the change can usually be submitted to the court and approved without a contested hearing. When one parent simply ignores the order, the remedy is enforcement.
