Child Support · Florida Guidelines

Child support that reflects the real numbers.

Florida calculates child support from both parents' net incomes, the number of children, the timesharing schedule, and the costs of health insurance and childcare, using guidelines set by statute.

The guideline formula is only as accurate as the numbers that go into it. McNamara Law represents parents who pay and parents who receive support, and we make sure income, overnights, and expenses are calculated correctly.

How It Is Calculated

The Florida child support formula

Florida uses an income shares model. The parents' net monthly incomes are combined, a guideline chart sets the basic support need for that income and number of children, and each parent is responsible for a share in proportion to their income.

The amount is then adjusted for health insurance, childcare, and the timesharing schedule. When a parent has the child for at least 20% of overnights in a year, the formula accounts for the time the child spends in each home.

  • Gross income includes wages, bonuses, overtime, self-employment income, and many benefits
  • Allowable deductions include taxes, health insurance, and court-ordered support actually paid for other children
  • Imputed income. A court can assign income to a parent who is voluntarily unemployed or underemployed
  • Deviations. A judge can depart from the guideline amount, but larger departures require written findings
How Long and How Far Back

When support starts and stops

Child support generally continues until a child turns 18. It can continue to age 19 if the child is still in high school and expected to graduate before turning 19, and longer for a dependent adult child with a disability.

In an initial case, a court can also award retroactive support for up to 24 months before the petition was filed, for the period the parents lived apart.

Good to Know

Rules that surprise parents

  • Child support belongs to the child. Parents generally cannot waive it by private agreement
  • Equal timesharing does not mean zero support when incomes are different
  • Support and timesharing are separate. Unpaid support is not a legal reason to withhold the child, and denied time is not a reason to stop paying
  • A new spouse's income is generally not counted
Changing or Enforcing an Order

When circumstances change, or payments stop

A support order stays in effect until a court changes it. If your income drops or the other parent's rises, you must file to modify. A change generally applies only from the date you file, so waiting costs money.

If the other parent stops paying, the court has strong tools to enforce the order.

  • Modification generally requires a substantial change and a difference of at least 15% or $50 a month, whichever is greater. More →
  • Income withholding directly from the paying parent's paycheck
  • Contempt of court for willful failure to pay
  • Driver's license suspension, liens, and interception of tax refunds
  • Judgments for arrears that accrue interest
