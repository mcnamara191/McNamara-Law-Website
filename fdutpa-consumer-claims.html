What Is FDUTPA?

Florida's main consumer protection law

The Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, Fla. Stat. §§ 501.201–501.213, makes it unlawful for a business to use deceptive acts, unfair practices, or unfair methods of competition in trade or commerce. It applies to car dealers, repair shops, contractors, and many other businesses.

A consumer who has suffered a loss because of a FDUTPA violation can sue for actual damages, which is often measured as the difference between the value of what you were promised and the value of what you actually got. The court may also award reasonable attorney's fees to the prevailing party.

* About feesFee awards under FDUTPA are discretionary and can run in either direction, so a business that wins may ask the court to make the consumer pay its fees. That is why an honest evaluation of your claim before filing matters.

  • A deceptive act or unfair practice. A representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, or one that is unethical, oppressive, or substantially injurious.
  • Causation. The deceptive or unfair practice caused your loss.
  • Actual damages. You lost money or got something worth less than what you paid for.
  • No proof of intent is required. Unlike common-law fraud, FDUTPA generally does not require proof that the business intended to deceive you.
  • Other claims may apply too. Fraud, breach of contract, breach of warranty, the Motor Vehicle Repair Act, and federal laws like the Odometer Act can be brought alongside FDUTPA.
Automobile Dealers

Common claims against car dealerships

Buying a car is one of the largest purchases most people make, and one of the most common sources of consumer complaints. These are some of the dealer practices we see most often.

Vehicle History

Undisclosed Damage

Prior accidents, frame damage, flood damage, or major mechanical problems the dealer knew about but didn't disclose, or actively concealed.

Title

Title & Branding Problems

Salvage, rebuilt, or flood-branded titles that weren't disclosed, former rental or lemon-buyback vehicles sold as something else, or a title the dealer can't deliver.

Odometer

Odometer & Mileage Fraud

Rolled-back or misstated mileage. Federal law provides enhanced damages for odometer fraud in addition to Florida claims.

Financing

Financing & "Yo-Yo" Sales

You drive the car home, then get called back days later and told the financing "fell through" and you must sign at a higher rate or lose your down payment or trade-in.

Add-Ons

Hidden Fees & Add-On Packing

Service contracts, GAP, protection packages, and fees added without clear disclosure or consent, or a price that doesn't match the advertised price.

Trade-In

Trade-In Payoff Failures

The dealer promised to pay off your trade-in loan and didn't, leaving you responsible for two car payments and damaging your credit.

Signed an "as is" form? That generally limits implied warranties. It does not give a dealer permission to lie about a vehicle or hide known problems. Florida also requires a written demand letter to a motor vehicle dealer at least 30 days before a FDUTPA lawsuit is filed, and we handle that step for you. For new vehicles with repeated defects, Florida's Lemon Law (Chapter 681) may also apply.

Mechanics & Repair Shops

Your rights under the Florida Motor Vehicle Repair Act

Florida's Motor Vehicle Repair Act, Fla. Stat. §§ 559.901–559.9221, sets specific rules that most repair shops must follow. A customer injured by a violation can sue for damages and, in many cases, attorney's fees and costs. Violations may also support a FDUTPA claim.

We represent consumers in disputes over:

  • Unauthorized repairs or charges you never approved
  • Bills far above the estimate without your consent
  • Unnecessary, incomplete, or defective work, or repairs that made the problem worse
  • Parts charged but never installed, or used parts billed as new
  • Damage to your vehicle while it was in the shop's care
  • Excessive storage fees and refusals to release your vehicle
Key Rights

What Florida repair shops generally must do

  • Written estimate. If you ask for repairs expected to cost more than $100, the shop generally must give you a written estimate unless you waive it in writing.
  • Limits on going over. The shop generally cannot charge significantly more than the estimate without your authorization before the extra work is done.
  • Itemized invoice. Your invoice must identify the work performed and the parts supplied, including whether parts are used or rebuilt.
  • Replaced parts. You can ask to have replaced parts returned to you, which is valuable evidence if the work is disputed.
  • Registration. Most repair shops must be registered with the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services.
Shop Won't Release Your Car?

Getting your vehicle back during a billing dispute

Florida law generally allows a repair shop to assert a lien on a vehicle for the reasonable cost of authorized work, which is why shops often refuse to release a car until the bill is paid. But a lien is not a blank check.

Under Florida law, a customer who disputes a repair bill can usually obtain the release of the vehicle by posting a bond with the clerk of court for the amount of the invoice. The vehicle is released, and the dispute over what is actually owed is decided by the court instead of by the shop holding your car hostage while storage fees grow.

  • Don't sign anything new just to get your keys back without understanding it. Releases and "acknowledgments" can waive claims.
  • Get the invoice in writing and compare it to the written estimate and any authorizations you gave.
  • Paying under protest may be an option, but document clearly that you dispute the charges.
  • Act quickly. Storage charges can accumulate daily, and a shop may eventually move to sell a vehicle under a lien.
  • Replevin may be available if someone is wrongfully keeping your vehicle or property without a valid lien. Learn about replevin →
What to Do Right Now

Five steps that protect your claim

What you do in the first days after discovering a problem can make or break a consumer case.

i

Keep everything

Contracts, buyer's orders, estimates, invoices, ads, window stickers, texts, emails, and voicemails. Screenshot online listings before they disappear.

ii

Document the problem

Photos, video, and an independent inspection or second opinion from another qualified mechanic.

iii

Put it in writing

Communicate in writing where possible, and keep your tone factual. Avoid signing releases or accepting partial "goodwill" offers before getting advice.

iv

Watch the deadlines

FDUTPA claims are generally subject to a four-year limitation period, but other claims and remedies can have shorter windows.

v

Talk to a lawyer

We'll review your documents, identify every available claim, and handle the pre-suit demand and negotiation.
